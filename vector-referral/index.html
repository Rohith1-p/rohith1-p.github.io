Vector Referral

A forward deployed engineering case study — healthcare referral AI, from discovery to a live clinical pilot in four months

Role: Founding AI Engineer (solo)  ·  Timeline: Sep 2025 – Apr 2026  ·  Domain: Dental referrals (US)  ·  Stack: Python, FastAPI, LLM APIs, Sikka, PostgreSQL, Railway/Vercel

When a general dentist refers you to a specialist, the referral goes out — and the report almost never comes back. I built the platform that closes that loop, with an LLM in the middle of it and a human signature on everything it writes.

On this page: At a glance · The problem · Discovery · The workflow split · The build · Grounding · Ontologies · Evaluation · Real people · Judgment calls · Outcome · Why FDE

§At a glance

Problem
Referral reports, X-rays, and treatment status never flow back to referring dentists — the care loop is broken.
Approach
Embedded with real offices first; split the workflow into deterministic code, AI drafting, and human sign-off.
Key techniques
Per-office schema mapping (an ontology layer), few-shot grounding, abstention design, RAGAS-style eval harness.
Outcome
Live pilot with real offices; reports reliable enough for a physician's signature; an incumbent in the dental space tested the product and expressed acquisition interest.

1The actual problem

In dental care, the referral workflow looks like this: a general dentist sends a patient to an endodontist, an oral surgeon, an orthodontist. The referral itself is the easy part. What happens next is the broken part:

  • The specialist's findings, X-rays, and treatment reports rarely make it back to the referring dentist — or arrive weeks later, by fax, after the patient has already returned.
  • The referring office finds out what happened by calling the specialist's office and asking. Every referral means phone tag between front desks.
  • The patient falls through the cracks when nobody calls: the loop of care silently breaks.

Everyone in the industry knows this. Nobody had fixed it, because the workflow runs across independent businesses — each with its own practice management system (PMS), its own report template, and no incentive to change tools for someone else's convenience.

This is not a modeling problem. It's a workflow problem that AI can help with — which is exactly why it interested me.

2How a forward deployed engineer approaches it

I didn't start with code. I started where the work actually happens.

Phase 0 — Discovery. I sat with the people in the workflow — dentists, specialists, front-desk staff — and had them walk me through the last referral they handled, step by step. Not "what do you usually do," but that specific one: where did the report get stuck, who called whom, what did you copy by hand. That's where the real workflow lives, not in any job description.

What the walkthroughs gave me:

  • The referral itself is not the pain — tracking the response is.
  • Specialists don't write reports back because nobody has time — not because they're stubborn. Any fix that adds work for the specialist is dead on arrival.
  • Every office has a different report template. "Just standardize the form" was never going to happen.
  • The workflow spans organizations that will never share a system — so the platform has to be the connective tissue, not another walled garden.

This is the FDE instinct: understand how the business actually operates before building anything. The product design fell out of the discovery almost mechanically.

3Dividing the workflow: deterministic, AI, human

The core design decision was where each kind of work belongs. I mapped every step of the referral lifecycle and sorted it into three kinds of work. Follow the numbers — one referral, seven steps, flowing left to right:

Steps 1–4
▣ Deterministic code
Must be exact, idempotent, and auditable — every single time. No probability allowed.
1Poll each office's PMS every 5–10 min for changed records (change-window filter, idempotent).
2Retrieve new documents — X-rays, clinical notes — and attach them to the referral record.
3Advance the referral state machine: sent → booked → visited → report due → signed → returned.
4Deliver the signed report and every status event back to the referring office, documents attached.
→
Steps 5–6
▣ AI drafting
Genuinely unstructured work — the part a human used to do by reading and typing. The LLM is a draftsman only.
5Read the extracted records and map every field into this office's own template (per-office schema mapping).
6Draft the referral report strictly from source documents — grounded in golden examples; any field with no source is flagged blank, never inferred.
→
Step 7
▣ Human accountability
In healthcare, a confident wrong report is worse than a late one.
7The specialist verifies, corrects, and digitally signs. Nothing AI-generated is ever sent to a referring office without a signature.

Step 7 is the one I'd defend hardest: the AI never sends anything. The failure mode I designed against was a plausible-looking fabricated field inside a clinical document. The human-in-the-loop gate isn't a limitation of the AI — it's the design that made real clinicians willing to touch it at all.

4The build, in phases

Phase 1 — Close the loop dumbly (weeks 1–6).
Before any AI: a referral portal where the loop itself closes — booking, visit, treatment-complete events flow back to the referring office automatically, documents attached. Deterministic only. Offices got value on day one, and I got a live workflow to learn from. I rejected bolting onto fax/email — those channels can't carry structured events.

Phase 2 — Put the LLM in the middle (weeks ~6–12).
Specialists had no time to write reports — the loop died at the report. So: integrate with each office's existing PMS (never ask an office to change tools), poll for changes every 5–10 minutes, extract new documents, and have an LLM draft the report from the source records. Specialist corrects and signs. Reports that never got written now flow — with a human accountable for every one.

Phase 3 — Earn the signature (weeks ~12–16).
The first drafts weren't good enough for a clinician's signature — this phase was about making them trustworthy. Grounding, schema mapping, abstention, and an eval harness (next two sections). This is where the product went from demo to something a physician would put their name on.

5Teaching the AI not to guess

The naive drafts had real failure modes, and naming them precisely was half the fix:

  • Hallucinated values — plausible clinical details that appeared in no record.
  • Transposed fields — similar field names on different template slots, swapped.
  • Schema variance — every office's template meant the same concept had a different name and shape.

The fixes, in layers:

  1. Few-shot grounding — 10–15 golden example reports per template, so the model drafts in the office's own voice and format.
  2. Explicit field definitions — every field defined in the prompt; nothing left to interpretation.
  3. Per-office schema mapping — each office's template mapped explicitly to the platform's canonical referral model (this became the ontology layer — next section).
  4. Abstention — the rule that mattered most: no source data → the field is flagged blank, never inferred. The AI is allowed to say "not found." A blank a human fills in is a workflow; a fabricated value in a clinical report is a liability.

The principle underneath all four: the model never gets to invent the ontology. It works with the nouns and verbs the customer actually uses, or it declines.

6Abstraction & ontologies: one platform, every office's vocabulary

The hardest engineering problem wasn't the LLM — it was variance across customers. Every office had its own template, its own field names, its own implicit model of a referral. Naively, that's an N-way special-case explosion.

What I built instead was a thin ontology layer: a canonical model of the referral domain (Patient, Referral, Specialist, Report, Procedure, Status, timestamps) with each office's schema mapped onto it. Onboarding a new office = writing their mapping, not rewriting the pipeline.

In forward-deployed terms: their nouns (entities and fields) and their verbs (workflow events) made explicit. The LLM grounds against the mapping; the deterministic state machine consumes it; the eval harness validates it.

I've since gone deep on this formally — RDF/OWL, SHACL validation, SPARQL — building a proper referral-domain ontology as a capstone, because this pattern (a customer-specific ontology is the difference between a generic model and a deployed one) is exactly where enterprise AI lives or dies.

7Evaluation: no iteration without a gate

"It seems better" is not a deployment criterion. I built an offline eval harness before tuning anything:

  • Golden set: 30–50 real (de-identified) documents from pilot offices.
  • Metrics: faithfulness of drafts to source records, context precision/recall per field (RAGAS-style), plus abstention correctness — did it flag what it should have flagged?
  • The rule: every prompt change, new guardrail, or model swap ran the full harness first. No iteration shipped without passing its gate.

This changed the character of development: hallucination fixes went from vibes to measurable deltas, and "good enough for a signature" became a threshold I could watch the system cross.

8Testing with real people

The pilot ran with real offices — not staging environments, not friendly demos:

  • Real referrals flowed, real reports got drafted, real specialists signed (or rejected and corrected) them.
  • Every correction by a specialist was a labeled example feeding back into golden sets and few-shot libraries — human feedback as a training signal.
  • I watched adoption honestly: the signature gate was the hinge. Specialists wouldn't touch an autonomous sender; they would absolutely review-and-sign a good draft, because it saved them the writing.
  • Result the offices felt: referring offices got status without phone calls — the loop the whole referral market had accepted as broken. Specialist reports came back roughly 40% faster across pilot offices — meaning treatment started earlier.

9Engineering judgment calls worth naming

Polling, not webhooks. The PMS ecosystem is legacy and on-prem; I integrated through Sikka (one API over 400+ PMS products). The non-obvious finding: even Sikka's webhooks only fire after their on-prem agent extracts data on its own schedule — so webhooks would have bought zero freshness. I chose polling with change-window filters (only records modified since last run), idempotent, every 5–10 minutes. Cost: ≤1 interval of latency — clinically irrelevant for referrals. Simplicity won because I understood where the freshness ceiling actually sat: upstream of either option.

Observability on a solo pilot. No ops team — so the system watched itself: referral success rates, error dashboards, drill-down to root cause, email alerts. I knew when it broke before the offices did. On a pilot, trust is the renewal; silent failures kill it.

HIPAA as design, not paperwork. BAAs with every vendor touching PHI; pseudonymized IDs in transit with identity mapping living only inside the platform; minimum-necessary content in patient SMS (status + portal link, never treatment details); scoped access per referral.

10Outcome

  • Live pilot with real offices, full loop closure: referral → booking → visit → AI-drafted, specialist-signed report → back to the referring dentist, automatically.
  • Reports trustworthy enough that clinicians put their signatures on them — the adoption bar that matters in healthcare.
  • The product validated well enough that an incumbent in the dental space tested it and expressed acquisition interest (interest, not an offer — the evaluation was paused at that stage).
  • Built end-to-end by one engineer in ~4 months: discovery, platform, integrations, AI layer, evals, compliance, observability.

The part I'd keep from this for any deployment: the model is the easy part. The work is understanding the workflow, drawing the boundaries between code and judgment, and earning the trust of the people whose names go on the output.
